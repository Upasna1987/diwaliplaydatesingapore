Rangoli is one of the easiest ways to bring Deepavali home, but classic coloured powder and a two-year-old are not a great mix. Diwali Playdate 2026 had more than 100 guests: 45-plus kids aged 1 to 10 and 55-plus adults. Running rangoli for that many little ones taught us quickly what works for small hands and what ends up on the carpet. This guide covers the ideas we'd use again, sorted by age, plus simple designs, a coloured-rice recipe and where to buy supplies in Singapore.

The short version
  • Under 2: flower petals only, on a big board or tray. No small grains.
  • 2 to 4: fill a big chalk or tape outline with coloured rice, dal or petals.
  • 4 to 6: printable rangoli sheets, stencils and simple symmetry.
  • 6 and up: a proper floor rangoli, a metre across, with parents helping.
  • Skip coloured powder with toddlers. It's messy and it stains hands.

What is rangoli? An explanation for kids

Rangoli is a bright, patterned design made on the floor at the entrance of a home during Deepavali and other festivals. Families make it to welcome guests and to invite Goddess Lakshmi, who brings good luck and prosperity, into the house. Rangoli can be made with coloured rice, flower petals, chalk, sand or coloured powder, and every one is different.

In South India, a similar tradition called kolam is drawn every morning with white rice flour, often as a pattern of lines looped around a grid of dots. If you walk around Little India in Singapore during the Deepavali season, you'll see both styles.

For a young child, the one-line version is: “We make a pretty pattern at the door so that happy things and happy people come inside.”

Rangoli for babies and young toddlers (under 2)

For our under-2s we laid a large board on the floor and let them place soft flower petals: marigold, rose and chrysanthemum petals pulled from garlands. Petals are big enough to be safer if they go in a mouth, they feel lovely, and the result looks festive even when it's completely random.

  • Pull the petals off a few garlands into separate bowls, one colour per bowl.
  • Draw one big circle with chalk or masking tape so there's a “place” to put them.
  • Let them drop, sort and scatter. That's the whole activity, and it can hold a toddler for a surprisingly long time.
  • Take a photo from above. Babies love seeing “their” rangoli afterwards.

Avoid rice, dal, beads and sequins for this age. They are choking hazards, which is why we kept the baby rangoli to flowers only.

Rangoli for toddlers (2 to 4): fill-the-shape rangoli

Two- and three-year-olds enjoy filling a shape far more than designing one. Draw a simple outline, such as a flower, a diya or a big circle with petals, and give them one material per section.

  • Coloured rice: bright, cheap and easy to sweep up (recipe below).
  • Dal and lentils: yellow moong, orange masoor, green moong and black urad give you four natural colours straight from the kitchen cupboard.
  • Glue rangoli: for less sweeping, paint glue inside the outline on card first, then sprinkle rice or dal on top. Shake off the extra and you have a keepsake for the front door.
  • Sticker rangoli: round dot stickers on a printed outline. Zero mess, great for a restaurant table or a long car ride.

Keep each section large, about the size of your child's palm. Small sections are frustrating at this age, and it's fine if the colours mix. That's part of the fun.

Rangoli for preschoolers (4 to 6): sheets, stencils and symmetry

From around four, kids start to notice patterns and enjoy making both sides match. This is a good age for:

  • Printable rangoli colouring sheets. Colour them in, or slip the sheet into a clear plastic sleeve or tray and fill the shapes with rice and petals. It becomes a reusable rangoli they can make again and again.
  • Stencils. Plastic rangoli stencils or a homemade cardboard one. Hold it flat, sprinkle, then lift slowly. The lift is the magic part.
  • Finish the other half. Draw half a design and let them copy the other side. It sneaks in some maths.
  • Dot rangoli. Draw a grid of dots and let them join the dots with curvy lines, the way kolam is made.

Rangoli for older kids (6 and up): a proper floor rangoli

This is what we're planning for the next Diwali Playdate. Draw a big pattern on the floor in chalk, at least a metre across, and mark each zone with a dab of washable paint to show its colour. Older kids fill the zones with coloured rice, dal or flowers while parents help. A few tips:

  • Put a plastic sheet or a large piece of cardboard down first. It makes moving and cleaning up much easier.
  • Start from the centre and work outwards, one ring at a time.
  • Use a funnel made from rolled paper, or a squeeze bottle, for neat lines.
  • Choose a low-traffic corner. Finished rangolis get trampled fast at a party.

5 easy rangoli designs for kids

  1. The big flower. One circle in the middle and eight teardrop petals around it. Perfect for toddlers.
  2. The diya. A bowl shape with a flame on top, surrounded by a circle of dots. Easy to recognise and very Deepavali.
  3. The lotus. Two rings of pointed petals, one behind the other, with a small circle in the middle.
  4. The sun. A circle with triangles all the way around it, then a ring of dots. Good for practising symmetry.
  5. The dot kolam. A 5 by 5 grid of dots, with curvy lines looped around them. Older kids can invent their own.

How to make coloured rice for rangoli

Coloured rice is our favourite rangoli material for kids. It's bright, it doesn't stain the way powder does, and it keeps for months in a jar.

  1. Put one cup of uncooked white rice in a zip-lock bag.
  2. Add about half a teaspoon of food colouring and one teaspoon of vinegar, which helps the colour stick.
  3. Seal the bag and let your child shake and squish it until all the grains are coloured. Kids love this part.
  4. Spread the rice on a tray lined with baking paper and leave it to dry for a few hours or overnight.
  5. Store each colour in a separate jar. Red, yellow, green, blue and pink look great together.

Salt works the same way and gives a sparkly, sandy look. Mix it with powdered chalk or a little poster paint and let it dry.

Where to buy rangoli supplies in Singapore

  • Little India: in the weeks before Deepavali, shops along Serangoon Road and around Campbell Lane sell rangoli powder, stencils, diyas and flower garlands. The Deepavali festival bazaar in Little India is a fun family outing in itself.
  • Flower garlands: the garland stalls in Little India sell marigold and jasmine garlands. Pull the petals off for a baby-safe rangoli.
  • Supermarkets: rice, dal, salt and food colouring are all you need for most kids' rangoli.
  • Craft and stationery shops: dot stickers, chalk, glue and card for the mess-free versions.

What we'd skip

In 2026 we tried small MDF rangoli boards with coloured powder. Honest review: the boards were too small, the powder went everywhere, and it stained little hands. Lovely for adults, not for under-5s. If you want to use powder, do it outdoors, with older kids, on a big surface.

Rangoli at home in Singapore: practical tips

  • Make it movable. If you live in an HDB flat or condo, make your rangoli on a large board, tray or mat that can sit outside the door for the evening and come back in. It keeps the corridor clear and survives the rain.
  • Fight the breeze. Corridors can be windy. Rice and dal stay put far better than powder.
  • Clean up with a dustpan, not a vacuum. Sort the rice back into jars to use again next year.
  • Keep diyas a safe distance away from rangolis made with dry petals or paper.

Frequently asked questions

What age can kids start making rangoli?

From about one year old with flower petals on a board or tray. From two, they can fill big shapes with coloured rice or dal under supervision. Most kids can make a simple symmetrical design by themselves from around six.

What is the easiest rangoli for kids?

A big flower: one circle in the middle with eight teardrop petals around it. Draw the outline in chalk and let your child fill each petal with a different colour of rice, dal or flower petals.

Is rangoli powder safe for toddlers?

Traditional rangoli powder can stain skin and clothes and isn't meant to be eaten, so we don't recommend it for toddlers. Flower petals, coloured rice made with food colouring, and chalk are gentler alternatives.

How do you make rangoli without powder?

Use flower petals, coloured rice, lentils, coloured salt, chalk or dot stickers. You can also print a rangoli outline and colour it in, or fill it with rice inside a clear plastic sleeve.

Why is rangoli made during Deepavali?

Rangoli is made at the entrance of the home to welcome guests and to invite Goddess Lakshmi, who brings good luck and prosperity, into the house during the festival of lights.

Where can I buy rangoli colours in Singapore?

Shops in Little India, especially along Serangoon Road and around the Deepavali festival bazaar, sell rangoli powder, stencils and garlands in the weeks before Deepavali. For kids, rice, dal, salt and food colouring from any supermarket work just as well.
